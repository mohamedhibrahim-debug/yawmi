Bottom line

The gap is real: no Arabic-native, Egyptian-voiced household planner has traction. But nobody has yet asked a real Egyptian woman whether she will open a planner daily, or whether "effort minutes" make sense to her. Test that in two weeks before building anything else.

Holds up

  • No Arabic-native household planner with traction
  • Egyptian women do 5.4 hours a day of unpaid care and domestic work
  • "Waiting time" exists in no competitor
  • Android-first and a light app fit the market

Unproven

  • She will use a planner daily instead of WhatsApp, paper or memory
  • "Effort minutes" and a daily capacity are understood
  • Anyone will pay, at any price
  • A web link alone can reach her

Broken or risky

  • Reliable alerts when the app is closed need a server, which clashes with "no account"
  • Two codebases of the same app
  • Ticking a washing task done cancels its countdown
  • Unfinished tasks from past days vanish

Features the market shows users value

Ranked by the market researcher from reviews, pricing and ratings. The tag shows where Yawmi stands today.

  1. Ready-made routines and templates that remove the blank page (FlyLady, Sweepy, Home Routines). Have
  2. Reminders that work when the app is closed. The most common failure complaint in the category. Biggest gap
  3. A shared household with tasks assigned to people; the main paid feature in Sweepy, Tody, Cozi and FamilyWall. Later
  4. A visual sense of how full the day is (Tiimo, Structured, Llama Life). Partial
  5. Capacity and overload warnings (Sunsama, Structured). Yawmi's core idea, applied to housework for the first time. Have
  6. Guilt-free replanning when the day slips (Structured Replan, Todoist reschedule). Gap
  7. Room or zone rotation for deep cleaning. Later
  8. Gentle, care-framed motivation instead of streaks (Finch, Tiimo, FlyLady). Have
  9. Light gamification and kids' chores (Tody's "Dusty", BusyKid). Risky, see devil's advocate
  10. Shopping lists and meal planning (Cozi's core). Later
  11. WhatsApp as a channel (Any.do sells WhatsApp reminders). Test as sharing first
  12. Privacy, no account, no ads. Have

White space Yawmi can own

  1. An Arabic-native household planner with an Egyptian voice. Confirmed by both researchers; the devil's advocate notes the niche may be empty because demand is unproven.
  2. Waiting time as a first-class idea. No studied app models effort while a washing machine, oven or dough works in the background.
  3. Housework as a real, plannable workload. The 5.4 hours a day is verified. Whether "see how much you really do" helps or feels like surveillance is untested.
  4. Privacy and offline by design, against ad-funded and account-first competitors. This conflicts with closed-app reminders, which need a server.
  5. A light app for budget Android phones, which carry about 83% of Egyptian mobile traffic.
  6. Day periods that could later be labelled by prayer ("after Dhuhr"). Using the names as labels costs nothing; computing exact prayer times is a liability (see devil's advocate).

UX patterns worth adopting

The UX expert's shortlist from 22 patterns, filtered by what the devil's advocate let stand.

Guilt-free carry-over

Structured, Todoist

Unfinished tasks move to tomorrow in one tap, with a summary that lists only what she did. Never red, never "overdue".

Capacity before the list

Sweepy Smart Schedule

She says how much she can do, and the list never exceeds it. Keep it to a one-tap default, not a daily gate.

The dirt is the opponent, not her

Tody, Finch

No streaks to break and no score of her day. Rewards, if any, are gentle and cosmetic.

Start from templates

Routinery, Tiimo

Recognizing an option beats recalling one, especially for low digital literacy. Avoid long quiz onboarding like Fabulous's 42 screens.

Today only, with a "now" cue

Tiimo

Show one day at a time and keep the running timer and waits pinned in view on phones.

Literal icons with text labels

Low-literacy UX research

Abstract glyphs must be learned. Pair pictures with words, and keep the layout identical every time (Microsoft Research).

Arabic text at 16px or larger

UAE design system

Arabic letterforms depend on dots and suffer at small sizes (UAE guidelines).

Correct right-to-left mirroring

Material, Mozilla

Mirror direction arrows and progress. Do not mirror play icons, checkmarks or numbers (Material).

MVP UX audit

Strong already: the gentle Egyptian copy, clean right-to-left code, the unique waiting-time concept, undo on delete, honest wording about when alerts work, and template-based empty states.

Critical

Ticking "start washing machine" done cancels the countdown she actually needs.

Keep the wait running after completion, and ask "الغسالة اشتغلت؟ نعدّ ٥٠ دقيقة ونقولك".

Critical

Unfinished tasks from past days vanish, while the week strip still shows them as counts.

Add a gentle "من امبارح" tray, and show counts only on today and future days. Codex's earlier build already has "bring old tasks forward".

High

On phones, the running timer and waits sit below the whole task list.

A pinned "now" strip under the top bar.

High

The task form asks for numbers, and hides the key "waiting time" setting.

Effort as tap-to-choose chips and a visible "فيها استنا؟" switch. Keep it simple; skip keyword magic for now.

High

Text is 10–13px in places, and Unicode symbols are used as icons.

16px base, nothing under 12px, a bundled Arabic font, and labelled SVG icons.

High

Deleting a routine has no undo and no confirmation.

Offer undo, or suggest pausing instead.

Medium

Checkbox and input borders fail non-text contrast, and the most-tapped control is only 30px.

Darker borders and 44px tap targets.

Medium

The day plan is read-only, the capacity bar shrinks as she works, and the wait-finished alert disappears after 3 seconds.

Actionable plan rows, a done-and-remaining bar, and a banner that stays until dismissed.

The hardest points

  1. The real competitor is WhatsApp, paper and memory, not Sweepy. No study shows how Egyptian women manage the household today.
  2. "Effort minutes" is untested. If she doesn't think of mopping as 30 effort minutes, capacity, the day plan and the log all fall together.
  3. A pure web app can't send reliable alerts when closed. Scheduled local notifications never shipped in browsers (Chrome), so it needs server push, which breaks "no account". A thin Android wrapper with local alarms is the cheapest reliable path.
  4. A link alone may not reach her. Competitors rank for the exact Arabic searches she would type on Play. A Play listing via an Android wrapper (TWA) belongs in the beta, for discovery and trust.
  5. The UX ideas conflict. A daily check-in fights "usable in 30 seconds"; keyword parsing is magic that low-literacy users can't see or fix; a two-lane timeline is a chart idiom. About 31% of Egyptian women were illiterate in 2016 data (CAPMAS via Amwal Al Ghad), so decide explicitly whether they are in the audience.
  6. Over-engineering for zero users. The plan already specifies a sync engine and database roles, and there are two codebases of the same app.

What to do next

Where all three agents agree, combined with the roadmap.

  1. Merge to one codebase this week. Keep the new MVP, port Codex's "bring old tasks forward", global undo and manual time entries, then archive the Merna App build.
  2. Fix the two critical bugs and the readability issues. Waits survive completion, a "من امبارح" tray, 16px text, 44px targets, a pinned "now" strip. About a week of work.
  3. Wrap it for Google Play with a thin Android shell (TWA), mainly so she can find and trust it, and to unlock local alarms later.
  4. Run the five tests with 8–10 Egyptian women for two weeks. Reuse the user-testing script from the Codex folder.
  5. Freeze everything else until tests 1 and 2 pass: cloud backup, sync, prayer times, gamification, WhatsApp reminders, the two-lane timeline.
  6. If tests 1 or 2 fail, pivot cheaply to a WhatsApp-shareable daily plan with a simple light / medium / heavy day, rather than polishing the current model.
